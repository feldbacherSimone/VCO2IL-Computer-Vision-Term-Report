\chapter{Vision Based Sign Language Recognition}
This chapter provides an overview of current applications of gesture recognition in sign language translation. The following section will elaborate on the task definition of sign language translation as well as highlight some challenges and how they are addressed with different technologies.
 
\section{Overview}
In the field of sign language translation there are two main goals. Sign language production \cite{saundersSigningScaleLearning2022} aims to generate visual output of a signing individual from an input phrase. Sign interpretation instead refers to the conversion of a video feed into either written or spoken output. This chapter focuses on interpretation. It can be further divided into \textit{Continuous Sign Language Recognition} (CSLR) and \textit{Sign Language Translation} (SLT), where the former aims to identify individual signs in a video feed and the latter aims to translate a full sentence into written or spoken language \cite{cihancamgozSignLanguageTransformers2020}.
 
\section{The Challenge of Sign Language Recognition}
Each sign language features unique grammar, vocabulary, and syntax. They are complete languages and exist separately from the spoken languages they share a region with. Instead of a 1-to-1 mapping of signs to words, the meaning of a phrase needs to be understood in context in order to create a correct translation.
 
Signs are also composed of more than just hand gestures. The meaning of a phrase can change based on facial expression and the position of elements relative to the body and each other. These aspects are referred to as \textit{non-manual markers} and ideally need to be captured alongside hand gestures for a system to translate correctly \cite{goldin-meadowGestureSignLanguage2017}. 
 
\subsection{Datasets}
Training and evaluating a model requires a dataset with annotations that can be compared against the model's translation. In addition to sentence-level translations, some older methods also require gloss-level annotation, which is considerably more time consuming to produce. Glosses are the written representation of a single sign within a sentence and act as the building blocks from which a full sentence is later constructed.
 
The \textit{RWTH-PHOENIX-Weather 2014T}\footnote{https://github.com/enhuiz/phoenix-datasets} dataset was the first publicly available dataset for use in SLT and features German Sign Language (DGS) with gloss-level annotation. The \textit{HOW2SIGN}\footnote{https://how2sign.github.io/} dataset instead contains American Sign Language (ASL) samples with translations in both spoken and written English. It also provides multiple camera angles of the same signer along with depth and skeletal data. This kind of multimodal data enables more robust recognition, similar to the multimodal approaches discussed in the previous chapter, and is increasingly what newer translation methods rely on.
 
\section{Current Approaches}
Generally, the problem of sign language translation can be split into three subtasks. First, the boundaries of individual sentences and statements need to be identified in the video feed. The lack of punctuation or shifts in tone of voice to mark such boundaries makes \textit{Sign Segmentation} a non-trivial task. \textit{Sign Recognition}, corresponding to CSLR, then describes the identification of individual linguistic elements from the video feed, where spatial context and non-manual markers need to be taken into account. Finally, the desired output format, usually text or speech, needs to be generated in the \textit{Sign Translation} step \cite{cihancamgozSignLanguageTransformers2020}.
 
Older approaches relied heavily on gloss representation as an intermediate step in this pipeline: a CSLR model first recognized the gloss sequence, and a separate text-to-text model then translated that sequence into spoken language. The following two subsections trace how this pipeline has evolved, first by learning recognition and translation jointly, and more recently by moving toward richer multimodal input processed through large pretrained language models.
 
\subsection{Sign Language Transformers}
The gloss-based two-step pipeline works reasonably well, but the intermediate gloss representation becomes a bottleneck, since the final translation can only be as good as the gloss recognition step, and glosses themselves are a simplified, incomplete representation of what is actually signed.
 
Camgoz et al. \cite{cihancamgozSignLanguageTransformers2020} address this with an architecture called Sign Language Transformers, which learns recognition and translation jointly instead of as two separate stages. The model consists of two connected transformer components: a Sign Language Recognition Transformer (SLRT), an encoder trained with a Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss to predict gloss sequences from video, and a Sign Language Translation Transformer (SLTT), a decoder that generates the spoken language sentence directly from the representations learned by the SLRT. Because both parts are trained together, the network still benefits from gloss-level supervision during training, but the final translation is no longer strictly limited to what the gloss recognition step can capture.
 
Evaluated on the PHOENIX14T dataset, this joint approach roughly doubled the translation performance of prior end-to-end video-to-text models and even slightly outperformed the earlier gloss-based translation approach that had been treated as an informal upper bound. This result supports the idea that direct video-to-text translation can eventually be more accurate than translation through a gloss intermediate, given a sufficiently capable model architecture and training setup.
 
\subsection{Large Sign Language Models}
Where Sign Language Transformers still operate on 2D video, more recent work has moved toward richer multimodal input and toward reusing pretrained large language models (LLMs) instead of training a translation model from scratch. Zhang et al. \cite{zhangLargeSignLanguage2025a} propose Large Sign Language Models (LSLM), which translate American Sign Language using 3D motion data instead of 2D video. The 3D representation, based on the SMPL-X body model, captures body, hand, and face motion jointly and is more robust to camera angle, lighting, and background than flat video, addressing the same non-manual marker and spatial context requirements discussed earlier in this chapter.
 
This motion is compressed into discrete tokens using a vector-quantized autoencoder and then mapped into the same embedding space used by a pretrained LLM, allowing sign motion and text to be processed together. The model is first pretrained for basic sign recognition and then instruction-tuned, so it can follow different phrasings of the translation task rather than a single fixed prompt. LSLM outperforms a baseline built on a general-purpose motion-language model, showing that combining multimodal 3D input with the linguistic knowledge already present in pretrained LLMs is a promising direction, rather than training narrow translation models from scratch.
 
Taken together, these two approaches illustrate a broader shift in the field: away from brittle, gloss-bottlenecked pipelines and 2D-only input, and toward joint end-to-end training on richer, multimodal representations of sign.
