\chapter{Introduction}
In recent years a number of advancements in processing and synthesising human language in spoken and written form have been made. 
Applications like automatic subtitles and AI voice overs have found wide adoption on social media platforms with users generally accepting the technology and accessibility it provides.
The focus so far has been on the conversion between aural and written representations of language. Naturally the question arises if such translations are also possible between other modalities of communication.

Sign language is the primary form of communication for deaf and hard of hearing people, many of them considering it their native language. Communication occurs entirely through visual stimuli in the form of hand gestures, facial expressions and body language. 
Despite historic evidence of sign language dating back to the Paleolithic age, it has only been recognized in the field of linguistics in the 1960s \cite{rubenSignLanguageIts2005}, \cite{goldin-meadowGestureSignLanguage2017}. Prior to this the believe was, that signing prevented the development of spoken language and was therefore a hindrance to the education of deaf children, a stigma that has persisted to this day. 
These misconecptions combined with the low number of sign language speakers has led to the exlusion of people who rely on this form of communication from many aspects of society. 
Machine translation between aural speach and signs could aid in briding this gap. This report will look into the current state of research in hand gesture recognition, which is a core part of many sign translation approaches and give an overview of state of art methods in the field of automatic sign language translation.
